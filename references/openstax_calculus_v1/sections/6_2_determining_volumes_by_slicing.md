### Volume and the Slicing Method

Just as area is the numerical measure of a two-dimensional region, volume is the numerical measure of a
three-dimensional solid. Basic geometric formulas already give the volume of a rectangular solid (V = lwh, length
times width times height), a sphere (V = (4/3) pi r^3), a cone (V = (1/3) pi r^2 h), and a pyramid (V = (1/3) A h,
where A is the base area). Although some of these formulas were originally derived using geometry alone, all of
them can also be obtained using integration.

The cross-section of a solid is defined as the intersection of a plane with the solid. A cylinder is any solid
that can be generated by translating a plane region along a line perpendicular to the region; that line is called
the axis of the cylinder. In this general sense a cylinder need not have a circular base, and all cross-sections
perpendicular to its axis are identical. To find the volume of a cylinder, multiply the area of the cross-section
by the height: V = A * h. For a right circular cylinder (a soup can), this becomes V = pi r^2 h.

If a solid does not have a constant cross-section, a definite integral can be used to find its volume instead.
The solid is sliced into pieces, the volume of each slice is estimated, and the estimates are added together. The
slices must be parallel to one another and must reconstitute the whole solid when put back together. Consider a
solid S extending along the x-axis, sliced by planes perpendicular to the x-axis (later material in the chapter
considers slicing in other directions; choosing the wrong slicing direction can make the resulting integral
needlessly messy, so the choice matters). Because the cross-sectional area is not constant, let A(x) denote the
area of the cross-section at the point x. Let P = {x_0, x_1, ..., x_n} be a regular partition of [a, b], and for
i = 1, 2, ..., n let S_i be the slice of S stretching from x_(i-1) to x_i. For an arbitrary point x_i* in
[x_(i-1), x_i], the volume of slice S_i can be approximated by V(S_i) ~= A(x_i*) delta x. Adding these
approximations together gives

V(S) ~= the sum from i = 1 to n of A(x_i*) delta x,

which is a Riemann sum. Taking the limit as n -> infinity gives

V(S) = the limit as n -> infinity of the sum from i = 1 to n of A(x_i*) delta x = the integral from a to b of
A(x) dx.

This technique is called the slicing method.

### Problem-Solving Strategy: Finding Volumes by the Slicing Method
1. Examine the solid and determine the shape of a cross-section of the solid (drawing a picture helps if one is
   not already provided).
2. Determine a formula for the area of the cross-section.
3. Integrate the area formula over the appropriate interval to get the volume.

This section assumes the slices are perpendicular to the x-axis, so the area formula is stated in terms of x and
the limits of integration lie on the x-axis; the three-step strategy itself, however, is valid regardless of how
the solid is sliced.

**EXAMPLE 6.6 (Deriving the Formula for the Volume of a Pyramid).** Geometry gives the volume of a pyramid as
V = (1/3) A h, or V = (1/3) a^2 h for a pyramid with a square base of side length a. Use the slicing method to
derive this formula.

Solution: Orient the pyramid along the x-axis with its apex at the origin and its square base, of side a, at
x = h. The base is a square, so the cross-sections are squares as well (step 1). By similar triangles, if s is
the side length of the cross-sectional square at position x, then s/a = x/h, so s = ax/h. The area of one of the
cross-sectional squares is therefore

A(x) = s^2 = (ax/h)^2 (step 2).

Then the volume of the pyramid is found by integrating from 0 to h (step 3):

V = the integral from 0 to h of A(x) dx = the integral from 0 to h of (ax/h)^2 dx = (a^2/h^2) the integral from 0
to h of x^2 dx = (a^2/h^2) [(1/3) x^3] evaluated from 0 to h = (1/3) a^2 h.

This is the formula that was being sought. (Independently checked: this is an algebraic derivation rather than a
numeric one, and each step above was re-verified symbolically; no numeric answer to round.)

### Solids of Revolution

If a region in a plane is revolved around a line in that plane, the resulting solid is called a solid of
revolution, and the line is called the axis of revolution. Solids of revolution are common in mechanical
applications, such as machine parts produced by a lathe.

**EXAMPLE 6.7 (Using the Slicing Method to Find the Volume of a Solid of Revolution).** Use the slicing method to
find the volume of the solid of revolution bounded by the graphs of f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 5, x = 1, and x = 4, and
rotated about the x-axis.

Solution: Because the solid was formed by revolving the region around the x-axis, the cross-sections are circles,
with radius given by f(x) (step 1). Using the area of a circle,

A(x) = pi r^2 = pi [f(x)]^2 = pi (x^2 - 4x + 5)^2 (step 2).

The volume is then (step 3)

V = the integral from 1 to 4 of pi (x^2 - 4x + 5)^2 dx = pi the integral from 1 to 4 of
(x^4 - 8x^3 + 26x^2 - 40x + 25) dx = pi [(1/5)x^5 - 2x^4 + (26/3)x^3 - 20x^2 + 25x] evaluated from 1 to 4 =
(78/5) pi.

The volume is 78 pi / 5 cubic units. (Recomputed independently: expanding (x^2 - 4x + 5)^2 gives
x^4 - 8x^3 + 26x^2 - 40x + 25; the antiderivative evaluates to 412/15 at x = 4 and 178/15 at x = 1, a difference
of 234/15 = 78/5, matching the book exactly.)

Note: Example 6.7 plays a transitional role in the book, showing that the general slicing method applies directly
to a solid of revolution before the disk method is formally named in the next subsection; its arithmetic pattern
is close to Example 6.8 below, differing mainly in that the bounding function is quadratic rather than a square
root and the bounds are two vertical lines rather than an interval endpoint plus the origin.

### The Disk Method

When the slicing method is applied to a solid of revolution, it is usually called the disk method, because the
slices used to approximate the volume are disks. A thin rectangle under a curve, revolved about the x-axis,
produces a representative disk; the full region under the curve, revolved about the x-axis, produces the solid of
revolution itself. Since V = the integral from a to b of A(x) dx, and the cross-sectional area is now known ahead
of time to be the area of a circle, this gives the following rule.

### Rule: The Disk Method
Let f(x) be continuous and nonnegative. Define R as the region bounded above by the graph of f(x), below by the
x-axis, on the left by the line x = a, and on the right by the line x = b. Then the volume of the solid of
revolution formed by revolving R around the x-axis is given by

V = the integral from a to b of pi [f(x)]^2 dx.

(Deviation note: the book labels this equation 6.3; the numbering is omitted here since it is not needed outside
the source text.)

Applying this rule to the region under f(x) = (x - 1)^2 + 1 over the interval [-1, 3], revolved about the x-axis
(the running example used to introduce this subsection), gives

V = the integral from -1 to 3 of pi [(x - 1)^2 + 1]^2 dx = pi the integral from -1 to 3 of
[(x - 1)^4 + 2(x - 1)^2 + 1] dx = pi [(1/5)(x - 1)^5 + (2/3)(x - 1)^3 + x] evaluated from -1 to 3 = (412/15) pi
cubic units.

(Deviation note: in the book this computation appears as a direct illustration of the disk-method rule just
stated, ahead of the next formally numbered example, rather than under its own EXAMPLE label; it is kept in the
same place here for faithfulness. Recomputed independently and confirmed: 412/15.)

**EXAMPLE 6.8 (Using the Disk Method to Find the Volume of a Solid of Revolution 1).** Use the disk method to find
the volume of the solid of revolution generated by rotating the region between the graph of f(x) = sqrt(x) and
the x-axis over the interval [1, 4] around the x-axis.

Solution:

V = the integral from 1 to 4 of pi [f(x)]^2 dx = the integral from 1 to 4 of pi [sqrt(x)]^2 dx = pi the integral
from 1 to 4 of x dx = (pi/2) x^2 evaluated from 1 to 4 = 15 pi / 2.

The volume is 15 pi / 2 cubic units. (Recomputed independently: (pi/2)(16 - 1) = 15 pi / 2; confirmed.)

So far, every example has concerned a region revolved around the x-axis, but a solid of revolution can be
generated by revolving a plane region around any horizontal or vertical line. When the axis of revolution is the
y-axis, the mechanics of the disk method are nearly the same, except the bounding function is expressed in terms
of y and the integration is carried out with respect to y.

### Rule: The Disk Method for Solids of Revolution around the y-axis
Let g(y) be continuous and nonnegative. Define Q as the region bounded on the right by the graph of g(y), on the
left by the y-axis, below by the line y = c, and above by the line y = d. Then the volume of the solid of
revolution formed by revolving Q around the y-axis is given by

V = the integral from c to d of pi [g(y)]^2 dy.

**EXAMPLE 6.9 (Using the Disk Method to Find the Volume of a Solid of Revolution 2).** Let R be the region bounded
by the graph of g(y) = sqrt(4 - y) and the y-axis over the y-axis interval [0, 4]. Use the disk method to find the
volume of the solid of revolution generated by rotating R around the y-axis.

Solution: Since the region is revolved around the y-axis rather than the x-axis, the representative disks are
horizontal rather than vertical, and the integration is with respect to y.

V = the integral from 0 to 4 of pi [g(y)]^2 dy = the integral from 0 to 4 of pi [sqrt(4 - y)]^2 dy = pi the
integral from 0 to 4 of (4 - y) dy = pi [4y - y^2/2] evaluated from 0 to 4 = 8 pi.

The volume is 8 pi cubic units. (Recomputed independently: pi(16 - 8) = 8 pi; confirmed.)

### The Washer Method

Some solids of revolution have cavities in the middle and are not solid all the way to the axis of revolution.
This can happen simply because of how the region of revolution is shaped with respect to the axis, because the
region of revolution is defined as the region between the graphs of two functions, or because an axis of
revolution other than the x-axis or y-axis has been selected. When the solid has a cavity, the slices used to
approximate the volume are washers (disks with holes in the center) rather than plain disks: the cross-sectional
area is the area of the outer circle minus the area of the inner circle.

### Rule: The Washer Method
Suppose f(x) and g(x) are continuous, nonnegative functions such that f(x) >= g(x) over [a, b]. Let R denote the
region bounded above by the graph of f(x), below by the graph of g(x), on the left by the line x = a, and on the
right by the line x = b. Then the volume of the solid of revolution formed by revolving R around the x-axis is
given by

V = the integral from a to b of pi [(f(x))^2 - (g(x))^2] dx.

**EXAMPLE 6.10 (Using the Washer Method).** Find the volume of a solid of revolution formed by revolving the
region bounded above by the graph of f(x) = x and below by the graph of g(x) = 1/x over the interval [1, 4] around
the x-axis.

Solution:

V = the integral from 1 to 4 of pi [x^2 - (1/x)^2] dx = pi [x^3/3 + 1/x] evaluated from 1 to 4 = 81 pi / 4.

The volume is 81 pi / 4 cubic units. (Recomputed independently: the antiderivative of x^2 - 1/x^2 is x^3/3 + 1/x;
at x = 4 this is 64/3 + 1/4 = 259/12, and at x = 1 it is 1/3 + 1 = 16/12, for a difference of 243/12 = 81/4;
confirmed.)

As with the disk method, the washer method can also be applied to solids of revolution generated by revolving a
region around the y-axis.

### Rule: The Washer Method for Solids of Revolution around the y-axis
Suppose u(y) and v(y) are continuous, nonnegative functions such that v(y) <= u(y) for y in [c, d]. Let Q denote
the region bounded on the right by the graph of u(y), on the left by the graph of v(y), below by the line y = c,
and above by the line y = d. Then the volume of the solid of revolution formed by revolving Q around the y-axis is
given by

V = the integral from c to d of pi [(u(y))^2 - (v(y))^2] dy.

(Deviation note: the book does not work a separate numbered example applying this y-axis washer rule; it moves
directly to an example with an axis of revolution other than either coordinate axis instead, restated next.)

Rather than an example of the washer method with the y-axis as the axis of revolution, the book next considers an
axis of revolution that is a line other than one of the two coordinate axes. The same general method still
applies, but describing the outer and inner radii requires more care.

**EXAMPLE 6.11 (The Washer Method with a Different Axis of Revolution).** Find the volume of a solid of revolution
formed by revolving the region bounded above by f(x) = 4 - x and below by the x-axis over the interval [0, 4]
around the line y = -2.

Solution: The volume formula cannot be applied directly, since the axis of revolution is not one of the
coordinate axes, but the cross-sectional area is still the area of the outer circle minus the area of the inner
circle. The outer radius is the distance from the axis y = -2 up to f(x), which is f(x) + 2 = (4 - x) + 2 = 6 - x.
The inner radius is the distance from the axis y = -2 up to the x-axis, a constant 2. Therefore

V = the integral from 0 to 4 of pi [(6 - x)^2 - 2^2] dx = pi the integral from 0 to 4 of (x^2 - 12x + 32) dx =
pi [x^3/3 - 6x^2 + 32x] evaluated from 0 to 4 = 160 pi / 3.

The volume is 160 pi / 3 cubic units. (Recomputed independently: at x = 4, x^3/3 - 6x^2 + 32x = 64/3 - 96 + 128 =
64/3 + 32 = 160/3; at x = 0 the antiderivative is 0; confirmed.)
